01 · Context

A battery project is two hard problems wearing one name.

Before the site exists, the question is what should we build, and are we allowed to? After it runs, the question is is it making money, and why not? The client answered both in spreadsheets, PDFs and people's heads, and nothing carried over from one to the other.

FeasibilityUse case, size, site screening
SpecificationChemistry, BMS, inverter, layout, codes
BuildPermits, procurement, construction
CommissionTesting, witness tests, energization
OperateDispatch, trading, O&M, value
Weeks of manual engineering, high rework riskFleet measured in MWh, managed in €

The client owned six operating storage sites across Portugal, Spain, France, Italy and Germany (480 MWh, 240 MW) and had a growing pipeline of US projects. Every new project started from a blank template, and every operating site reported availability in megawatt-hours while the board asked about margin in euros.

I was brought in to design one product. Three weeks into discovery, it was clear the two problems shared a root: the decisions made during specification were the same facts operations needed later, from usable capacity and degradation curves to warranty limits and inverter capability. They were simply lost at handover.

“We spend three weeks writing a spec, hand it to EPC, and two years later my asset managers are reverse-engineering it from O&M manuals to work out why a site is under-earning.”Head of Asset Management, discovery interview

02 · The problem

Interdependent decisions, fragmented ownership, and no single view of value.

Specify

Every choice moves every other choice

System size, chemistry, BMS, inverter, footprint, enclosures, fire safety and interconnection are interdependent. Change the duration and the chemistry shortlist, enclosure count and fire separation all move.

Select

Trade-offs nobody compared the same way twice

Cost, safety, degradation, cycle life, temperature range, warranty terms and utility acceptance were weighed in a different spreadsheet each time, by whoever had the time.

Comply

Rules that change at every border

Federal, state, utility, fire code, environmental and permitting requirements vary by location and application. One missed obligation means rework, delay or failed commissioning.

Execute

Plans split across six teams

Engineering, procurement, permitting, utility coordination, construction and commissioning each kept their own plan. No one held the step-by-step roadmap.

Operate

Measured in MWh, managed in euros

Dashboards showed availability and state of charge. Nobody could say which site was losing money, to what cause, or which action would recover it.

Scale

Site two cost as much as site one

Without a shared asset model, each new site meant rebuilding data pipelines, reports and analysis from scratch.

Design challenge: How might we turn basic project information into a specification engineers trust enough to sign, and carry those decisions into a fleet view that runs on euros?

What we learned

Insight 1

Engineers don't want an answer. They want a defensible draft.

No engineer would sign a spec a machine wrote. Every one of them would happily edit a good draft, as long as they could see where each number came from.

Insight 2

Missing inputs caused more rework than wrong ones.

In the RFI logs we reviewed, most late changes traced back to something nobody asked early: fire setbacks, the utility's ride-through rules, soil bearing, or the enclosure's cold-weather rating.

Insight 3

"Recommended" without "for what" was ignored.

A chemistry ranked "best" was distrusted. The same chemistry described as "best for 4-hour firming at this site, because…" was accepted.

Insight 4

Operators think in euros per decision, not KPIs per page.

Asset managers wanted three things: what we're making, where we're losing it, and what to do on Monday.

“Show me the clause, not the conclusion. If I can't point the fire marshal to a section number, it doesn't exist.”Permitting lead, US development team

04 · Approach

Seven steps, one asset model from spec to dispatch.

We ran the work as a framework the client could repeat for every new site and region. Each step had a design deliverable, not just an engineering one.

Principle

Write once, use for the asset's whole life

Every decision captured in the Spec Copilot (rated power, usable energy, warranty cycle limits, degradation curve, inverter capability) becomes a field in the asset information model the Control Plane reads after commissioning. Nothing is re-keyed at handover.

  1. Set the business objectivesAvailability in MWh and €, deviation from theoretical performance, shadow costs, cycle profitability.Design: a shared KPI glossary, signed off by finance and operations
  2. Identify the key users and decisionsDevelopers, permitting, procurement, asset managers, dispatch & trading, O&M, analysts.Design: role-to-decision map (above)
  3. Prioritize the business KPIsFleet and cycle profitability (€/MWh, €/cycle), LCOS and ROACE.Design: cut from 40 candidate metrics to 4 headline KPIs
  4. Map data sources and reuse what existsBMS/EMS, CRM/ERP, GIS and market APIs fed into PI. No new sensors or databases.Design: a data-availability matrix behind every screen
  5. Structure and contextualize the dataAsset information model, ontology instantiation, templates so site two costs a fraction of site one.Design: the spec package is the template
  6. Build the analytics enablersCost, revenue and degradation models behind every euro on the dashboard.Design: every number opens its own calculation
  7. Deliver the MVP, then scaleA 10-week fast track on 1–2 representative sites, with a modular design to add sites and regions.Design: component library and region packs

07 · Decisions that shaped it

What we kept, and what we dropped.

Tested across 4 rounds with 11 engineers and operators, using real past projects so they could compare our output with what they had actually built.

  • KeptEvery AI statement shows its source, confidence and owner.Trust went up when people could see where a claim came from. Regulations cite the clause, the edition the jurisdiction has adopted, and the date it was last checked.
  • KeptMissing inputs block, they don't guess.If site area is unknown, layout and fire setbacks say "on hold" and point to the input that unblocks them. Engineers called this the most useful thing in the product.
  • KeptSign-off section by section.The AI drafts and a named engineer approves. Edits are tracked, so the review becomes the audit trail.
  • DroppedConversational intake.Slower to review and impossible to scan. Chat survived as a side panel for asking why the Copilot assumed something.
  • DroppedA single overall score per chemistry.Reviewers didn't trust a number that hid its weighting. It became fit for this use case, with a written rationale.
  • KeptEuros first, megawatt-hours second.In the Control Plane, every availability loss carries a shadow cost. O&M tickets started being ranked by value lost.
  • DroppedA 40-tile operations dashboard.Replaced by four headline KPIs, three diagnostic views and a ranked action list. Time to answer "which site needs attention" fell from minutes to seconds in testing.

        Benefit

        Consistency by design

        One repeatable analysis process covers sizing, chemistry, BMS, inverter, layout and regulatory screening, so two engineers on two continents now produce comparable specs.

        Benefit

        Risk surfaced earlier

        Missing inputs, unsuitable options, space limits, code requirements and interconnection issues are flagged before detailed engineering begins, when changing course is still cheap.

        13 · Reflection

        What I'd do differently.

        Bring the fire marshal in earlier

        We designed the compliance view with permitting leads. The people who actually read the output are authorities having jurisdiction. One session with an AHJ in week two would have reshaped the export format sooner.

        Design the "no" as carefully as the "yes"

        The most valued moments were when the Copilot said I can't answer this yet. I'd prototype refusals and on-hold states from day one, not discover them in round three.

        Track whether actions paid off

        We shipped the action list before tracking what accepted actions actually earned. Closing that loop is what turns a Copilot from interesting into trusted.
