Northfield Clinic · Today

On hold00:23

Pick a time with Dr. Rao

Pick a time. Nothing else is asked for yet.

Exhibit 2.1

The patient

Tuesday, 07:42 · fourth attempt

Ravi has called the clinic three times before work. The hold music is the only thing that answers. He doesn’t want an app. He wants to know whether anyone can see him this week.

The decision

Show real availability first, before asking who you are. Booking became three taps, and the morning phone queue fell by 41%.

Read the full case studyCareLine · Healthcare · 9 months
Project
CareLine, online booking for Northfield Clinics, 14 practices
My role
Lead UX designer
Team
2 designers, 1 researcher, 6 engineers
Method
22 interviews, two weeks in waiting rooms and on reception phones

01 · The problem

The phone line was the only door, and it was open for 40 minutes a day.

More than 300 people called at 07:00 for the same handful of appointments. Older patients, carers and shift workers were hit hardest, because they had no other way in.

02 · What the research said

Most callers already knew what they needed. What they lacked was visibility: nobody could see whether an appointment existed before committing to the queue. In the old online flow, 40% left before they ever saw a time slot.

“I book at 7am because that’s when I’m brave enough to call.”Participant 07, age 68

03 · What we tried

  • KeptAvailability before identity. Real slots are shown first, and details are asked for only once a time is chosen.
  • DroppedBooking by chatbot. Three of eight participants gave up partway through the conversation.
  • DroppedA paid reminder upgrade on the confirmation screen. I argued for it, and I was wrong.

04 · What shipped

A three-tap booking flow, one reminder the evening before, and symptoms described in the patient’s own words. The phone line stayed open for the people who need it most.

05 · What changed

  • −41%calls to the morning queue within three months
  • 3 tapsto book from the home screen, down from 11
  • 1,000patients a day booking without calling, by month six

06 · What I’d do differently

I’d bring receptionists into co-design from week one, not week six. They knew the edge cases better than any dataset.

Aria’s medicine · Today

Tap a dose each time it is given

Each tap cycles: not yet, Sam, Priya.

Exhibit 2.2

The parent

Thursday, 02:10 · day three of antibiotics

Priya is standing in a dark kitchen with a syringe of medicine. Did Sam give the 8 o’clock dose? The note on the fridge doesn’t say. A double dose is worse than a missed one.

The decision

Design for the household, not the individual. One shared timeline that answers a single question at 2am: has it been given?

Read the full case studyCareLine Household · Healthcare · 10 weeks
Project
Shared medicine timeline, an extension of CareLine
My role
Lead UX designer
Team
1 designer, 1 researcher, 3 engineers, 1 pharmacist
Method
Two-week diary study with 12 households, 6 home visits

01 · The problem

Medicine is given by a household, but every tool assumed one person.

A course of antibiotics for a child passes between two or three adults over several days. The record lived on fridge notes, in text messages and in memory, and it failed at exactly the moment it mattered: at night, when one adult was asleep.

02 · What the research said

Nobody wanted reminders. They wanted an answer to one question, has it been given, without waking anyone up. Eight of twelve households described at least one near miss in the past year.

“Nobody in my house trusts an app to remember. The calendar stays on the fridge.”Participant 15, household of four

03 · What we tried

  • KeptOne shared list with a name on every dose, so the answer is readable in a glance.
  • DroppedPer-person accounts with notifications to the other adult. It woke people up and still left the question open.
  • DroppedA dose calculator. The pharmacist showed us it moved risk from the label to our screen.

04 · What shipped

A single timeline per child, visible to every adult in the household. One tap records a dose and who gave it. A dose can be corrected, and the correction is shown, never hidden.

05 · What changed

  • 2 tapsto record a dose, from a locked phone
  • 71%of households had a second adult join in the first week
  • 0double doses reported in the eight-week pilot

06 · What I’d do differently

I’d test with grandparents and childminders sooner. The household was always bigger than the two parents we recruited.

Exhibit 2.3

The commuter

Monday, 07:58 · 90 seconds to decide

Tom is walking fast, coffee in one hand, phone in the other. The platform has changed. He has one glance, maybe two, before he chooses a staircase.

The decision

Remove everything that isn’t the next action. One number, one instruction, one countdown. Fewer screens, read faster.

Read the full case studyKingsford Line · Transport · 5 months
Project
Live departures for the Kingsford Line app
My role
Product designer
Team
1 designer, 1 content designer, 4 engineers
Method
30 shadowed commutes, glance tests at 1.5 seconds

01 · The problem

The departure board was complete, accurate and unreadable at walking speed.

The app copied the station board: every service, every platform, every note. A platform change appeared as one more row among many, at the moment when people had the least attention to spare.

02 · What the research said

People walking through a station look at their phone for about a second and a half at a time. In glance tests, fewer than half could name their platform from the old screen after one look.

“I don’t read it. I look for my number, and if I can’t find it I follow the crowd.”Commuter, shadowed journey 11

03 · What we tried

  • KeptOne next action for the journey you are on: platform, walking time, which way to turn.
  • DroppedColour-coded rows on the full board. Faster to scan, still too much to scan.
  • DroppedPush alerts for every platform change. People had muted them by day three.

04 · What shipped

A “next step” view that opens by default during a saved commute, with the full board one tap away. Platform changes replace the instruction on screen instead of adding a line to it.

05 · What changed

  • 3 secto find the right platform, down from 11
  • −27%missed-connection complaints in the first quarter
  • 1 screeninstead of four for a changed platform

06 · What I’d do differently

I’d design the full board and the next step together. Shipping them a release apart made the simple view feel like a mode, not the default.

Sunday, 23:16 · Flood claim

Upload the photos of the damage

Tap upload to see what Grace sees, three times.

Exhibit 2.4

The person under stress

Insurance claim, after a flood

Grace has photographed every ruined room. The upload fails for the third time. Stress narrows attention, so “something went wrong” reads as “you did something wrong.”

The decision

Errors should lower someone’s heart rate. Say what is safe first, then what happened, then exactly one next step, and a human name.

Read the full case studyFairweather Insurance · Claims · 4 months
Project
Home claims journey for Fairweather Insurance
My role
Senior product designer
Team
1 designer, 1 content designer, 5 engineers, claims handlers
Method
14 recorded claim calls, 9 interviews after a claim

01 · The problem

The error messages were written for the system, at the worst hour of someone’s year.

After a flood, people upload dozens of large photos on a weak connection. Uploads failed often, and every failure said the same thing: something went wrong. Many people assumed their whole claim was gone and started again from the first page.

02 · What the research said

On the recorded calls, the first question was almost never about the error. It was “have I lost everything?” People under stress read a vague message as blame, and a blank one as loss.

“I didn’t know if I’d broken it or if it had broken. I just closed it and cried.”Claimant, interview 4

03 · What we tried

  • KeptA fixed order for every error: what is safe, what happened, one next step, a named person.
  • DroppedA friendlier tone on the same vague message. Warm words with no information tested worse.
  • DroppedError codes for support. Useful to us, frightening to read.

04 · What shipped

Uploads now retry by themselves and save after each photo. When something does fail, the message starts with what has been kept, and offers a direct line to a handler who can already see the claim.

05 · What changed

  • 81%of claims completed online, up from 58%
  • −33%repeat calls about the same claim
  • 0claims restarted from page one after an upload error

06 · What I’d do differently

I’d fix the upload before rewriting the message. The best error is still the one nobody has to read.

Exhibit 2.5

The reader

Wednesday, 16:20 · glasses in the other room

Margaret is 74 and keeps her phone’s text at the largest size, because that is the size she can read. She opens her bank to check that her pension has arrived. The balance is there. She just can’t see the end of it.

The decision

Design at 200% first, then scale down. A layout that survives the largest text survives everything smaller.

Read the full case studyHarbour Bank · Accessibility · 6 months
Project
Large-text redesign of the Harbour Bank mobile app
My role
Senior product designer, accessibility lead
Team
2 designers, 6 engineers, 1 accessibility specialist
Method
11 sessions with customers aged 65 and over, audit of 38 screens at 200%

01 · The problem

The app worked perfectly at the one text size its designers used.

Every screen had been designed and reviewed at the default size. At the largest system setting, balances were cut off mid-number, buttons overlapped, and two screens could not be completed at all.

02 · What the research said

Almost a quarter of customers had increased their phone’s text size. They were not a special case. They were one of the largest groups we had, and they were the most likely to phone a branch instead.

“I can see there’s money in there. I just can’t see how much.”Customer, age 74, session 6

03 · What we tried

  • KeptDesigning at 200% first, then scaling down. Rows stack, amounts wrap, nothing has a fixed height.
  • DroppedA separate “simple mode”. People did not want a different app, they wanted this one to listen to their phone.
  • DroppedCapping text at 130% to protect the layout. It protected the layout and nobody else.

04 · What shipped

Every core journey rebuilt on a reflowing layout, with large text checked in design review and in automated tests. No setting to find: the app follows the size the phone is already set to.

05 · What changed

  • 38 of 38core screens usable at 200% text, up from 9
  • 23%of customers use a larger text size
  • −18%branch calls asking for a balance

06 · What I’d do differently

I’d put large text in the definition of done from the first sprint. Retrofitting 38 screens cost far more than building them right.

Exhibit 2.6

The nurse

Friday, 06:50 · ten minutes to handover

Jo has six patients to hand over and ten minutes to do it. A call bell goes off halfway through bed 12. When she comes back, the screen has timed out and the form is empty.

The decision

Assume every task will be interrupted. Save each step as it is made, and bring people back to the exact place they left.

Read the full case studyWard Handover · Clinical tools · 7 months
Project
Nursing handover for a 420-bed hospital
My role
Lead UX designer
Team
1 designer, 1 clinical lead, 4 engineers
Method
6 night shifts shadowed, 41 handovers observed

01 · The problem

The form assumed ten quiet minutes. Nobody on a ward has ten quiet minutes.

Handover was a single long form that saved only at the end and timed out after three minutes. Nurses were interrupted partway through most handovers, came back to an empty screen, and went back to paper.

02 · What the research said

We counted an interruption roughly every six minutes. The paper sheet survived because it stayed exactly as it was left. The software lost to a piece of paper on the one thing that mattered.

“I’ll do it properly on paper and badly on the computer, because paper waits for me.”Staff nurse, night shift 3

03 · What we tried

  • KeptEach check saves the moment it is confirmed, and the screen reopens where you stopped.
  • DroppedA longer timeout. It hid the problem for five more minutes and raised a privacy concern.
  • DroppedVoice entry. Wards are loud, and patients are listening.

04 · What shipped

Handover as a short list of checks per patient, each saved separately. After an interruption the nurse returns to the exact check, with a marker that says so. Paper sheets were withdrawn by the wards themselves.

05 · What changed

  • −62%handover items left blank
  • 9 minaverage handover per nurse, down from 14
  • 0forms lost to a timeout since launch

06 · What I’d do differently

I’d shadow night shifts before day shifts. The hardest conditions were where the real design brief was.

Exhibit 2.7

The sender

Saturday, 19:05 · first transfer abroad

Amara is sending £200 to her mother in Lagos for the first time. Her thumb hovers over Send. The app asks “Are you sure?”, which is the one question she can’t answer.

The decision

Replace the warning with a way back. A ten-second undo does what a confirmation box only pretends to do.

Read the full case studySendwell · Fintech · 5 months
Project
First-transfer journey for Sendwell, international payments
My role
Product designer
Team
1 designer, 1 researcher, 5 engineers, compliance
Method
18 interviews with first-time senders, funnel analysis of 40,000 transfers

01 · The problem

We asked “Are you sure?” at the moment people were least able to answer.

A first transfer abroad feels irreversible, and the app agreed. The confirmation screen repeated the warning and offered no new information. Nearly four in ten first-time senders stopped there.

02 · What the research said

People were not unsure about the amount. They were afraid of a mistake they would only discover afterwards. Several had sent a small test payment first, paying the fee twice for peace of mind.

“It asked if I was sure. If I was sure I wouldn’t be reading it three times.”First-time sender, interview 12

03 · What we tried

  • KeptA ten-second undo after sending, while the money has not yet left the account.
  • DroppedA second confirmation with the details repeated. More to read, same fear.
  • DroppedTyping the recipient’s name to confirm. Accurate, and exhausting on a phone.

04 · What shipped

One review screen, then send. A clear countdown offers a way back, and the recipient’s name is checked against the receiving bank before the button is enabled.

05 · What changed

  • 78%of first transfers completed, up from 61%
  • −44%support contacts about a wrong recipient
  • 2.1%of transfers undone, almost all for a mistyped amount

06 · What I’d do differently

I’d involve compliance at the sketch stage. Their concern was valid, and the hold period that answered it was their idea.

    Read the full case studyFieldnote · Public health · 8 months
    Project
    Fieldnote, visit records for community health workers
    My role
    Lead UX designer
    Team

    Method

    AI agent · Panel for a senior backend engineer

    Choose what the agent should weigh

      The agent starts with skills and availability only. Switch on interview load and watch the panel change.

      Exhibit 2.9 · AI

      The hiring lead

      Monday, 09:15 · 12 roles open, 3 panels short

      Nadia needs a three-person panel for a backend role by Thursday. She knows who is good: the same five people she always asks, who are also the most exhausted. An AI agent offers to choose for her. She won’t trust a list she can’t question.

      The decision

      The agent recommends and the recruiter decides. Every suggestion shows its reasons, every factor can be switched off, and spreading the load is on by default.

      Read the full case studyPanelwise · AI · HR tech · 6 months
      Project
      Panelwise, an AI agent that recommends interview panels, for a hiring platform running 2,400 interviews a quarter
      My role
      Lead product designer
      Team
      2 designers, 1 researcher, 1 data scientist, 5 engineers, the people team
      Method
      16 interviews with recruiters and hiring managers, an audit of 9,000 past panels, 3 rounds of Wizard-of-Oz tests

      01 · The problem

      Panels were put together from memory, and memory picks the same people.

      Recruiters spent around 40 minutes per role finding interviewers who had the right skills, were free, and had not been asked too often. One in eight interviewers carried a third of all interviews. Panels were rescheduled often, and candidates noticed.

      02 · What the research said

      Recruiters did not want the AI to choose for them. They wanted it to do the searching and show its working. In Wizard-of-Oz tests, unexplained suggestions were overridden 70% of the time. The same suggestions with reasons attached were accepted three times as often.

      “If it can’t tell me why Priya, I’ll pick Priya myself, and at least I’ll know why.”Recruiter, interview 5

      03 · What we tried

      • KeptRanked suggestions with the top reasons in plain words, and factors the recruiter can switch on and off.
      • KeptInterview load on by default, so the same experts are not booked every week.
      • DroppedBooking the top panel automatically. Fast, but nobody owned the choice, and interviewers declined more invites.
      • DroppedA single match score with no breakdown. People trusted it until it was wrong once, then stopped trusting it altogether.

      04 · What shipped

      The agent suggests a panel with reasons, availability already checked, and a warning when someone is overloaded. The recruiter changes the factors, swaps anyone and books. Every override is logged and used to improve the next suggestion. Names and personal characteristics are never used for ranking. Perspective comes from team, function and each interviewer’s own stated focus.

      05 · What changed

      • 6 minto put a panel together, down from 40
      • −52%interview load on the 20 busiest interviewers
      • 84%of suggested panels booked, with or without edits

      06 · What I’d do differently

      I’d treat the override log as a product feature from day one. Recruiters’ edits were the best training signal we had, and we started collecting them late.

      AI symptom navigator · Sunday, 23:40

      Question 1 of 3

      Is Yusuf showing any of these?

        Exhibit 2.10 · AI

        The late-night searcher

        Sunday, 23:40 · 38.9°C and rising

        Leila’s son has a fever and won’t settle. The out-of-hours line has a 45-minute wait. A search engine has already offered her meningitis, sepsis and a teething gel. She doesn’t need a diagnosis. She needs to know where to go, and how fast.

        The decision

        The AI chooses a level of care, never a diagnosis. Fixed rules check for danger signs first, and when the model is unsure, it moves you up a level and tells you why.

        Read the full case studyWaypoint · AI · Healthcare · 10 months
        Project
        Waypoint, AI symptom navigation for a regional health service’s urgent care line
        My role
        Lead UX designer
        Team
        2 designers, 1 content designer, 3 clinicians, 1 ML engineer, 6 engineers, a clinical safety officer
        Method
        24 interviews, 300 call recordings reviewed with nurses, safety testing against 1,200 clinical scenarios

        01 · The problem

        People searched their symptoms at night, and the internet answered with the worst case.

        The out-of-hours line had waits of up to 45 minutes. People either went to A&E with minor problems, or waited at home with serious ones because they did not want to make a fuss.

        02 · What the research said

        People were not asking for a diagnosis. They wanted to know where to go and how fast. Nurses always asked about danger signs first, never about likely causes, and that order became the backbone of the design.

        “I don’t need to know what it is. I need to know if I should be worried tonight.”Parent, interview 9

        03 · What we tried

        • KeptAnswers framed as a level of care and a time, such as “speak to a nurse within an hour”, never as a diagnosis.
        • KeptDanger signs asked first and handled by fixed clinical rules that the model cannot override.
        • KeptWhen the model’s confidence is low, it moves the person up one level of care and says so.
        • DroppedFree-text chat. It felt friendly, but people described symptoms vaguely and the model filled the gaps with guesses.
        • DroppedA list of possible conditions with percentages. People read it as a diagnosis, and it made them more anxious.

        04 · What shipped

        A short guided set of questions, danger signs first. Every outcome shows the level of care, why, what to watch for, and one tap to reach a person. Each answer says whether it came from a safety rule or the model. Clinicians review a sample of conversations every week.

        05 · What changed

        • 4 minmedian time to advice, against up to 45 minutes on hold
        • −23%non-urgent A&E visits in the pilot area
        • 0emergencies missed in safety testing across 1,200 scenarios

        06 · What I’d do differently

        I’d test with people who speak English as a second language much earlier. Our plain language was plainer to us than it was to them.

        AI research copilot · Study 14, 12 interviews

        Pick a theme to see what it stands on

        Exhibit 2.11 · AI

        The researcher

        Thursday, 17:30 · readout at 10 tomorrow

        Kemi has twelve interview transcripts and a readout in the morning. An AI tool offers her eight themes in thirty seconds. They read well. She has no idea which of them she could defend when the product lead asks, “Who said that?”

        The decision

        No theme without its evidence. Every finding links to the people who said it, and thin evidence is marked, not hidden.

        Read the full case studySynthesis · AI · Research operations · 6 months
        Project
        An AI research copilot for a 40-person UX team in a product company
        My role
        Lead product designer
        Team
        2 designers, 2 researchers, 1 ML engineer, 4 engineers
        Method
        12 researcher interviews, 8 synthesis sessions observed, tested against 3 past studies with known findings

        01 · The problem

        Synthesis took longer than the research, and the findings rarely left the room.

        Researchers spent two to three days tagging transcripts for every study. Past studies were buried in folders, so product teams commissioned research that already existed.

        02 · What the research said

        Researchers were not afraid of AI writing themes. They were afraid of themes they could not defend in front of a sceptical product manager. Every finding needed a quote, and a person behind the quote.

        “A theme without a quote is just my opinion with better formatting.”Senior researcher, interview 3

        03 · What we tried

        • KeptEvery theme links to the exact quotes and participants that support it, and shows how many.
        • KeptThemes with thin evidence are marked, never hidden and never promoted.
        • DroppedA one-click executive summary. It read well, flattened nuance, and got quoted out of context.
        • DroppedAuto-tagging that rewrote researchers’ own tags. They lost ownership of their codebook.

        04 · What shipped

        The copilot drafts themes from transcripts, links each one to its evidence, and flags themes with a single source. Researchers merge, rename or reject every theme before anything is shared. A study library answers “what do we already know?” using past research only, with every answer traced to a study.

        05 · What changed

        • 1 dayto synthesise a study, down from 3
        • 2.4×more past studies reused by product teams
        • 0themes shared without a linked quote

        06 · What I’d do differently

        I’d bring product managers into testing earlier. They were the real audience for the evidence links, and they asked for things researchers never would.

        FSM copilot · Job 4471 · Roof plant room

        Fault E-47

        Likely causes for this chiller

          Exhibit 2.12 · AI

          The engineer

          Wednesday, 14:10 · on a roof, gloves on

          Ollie is two years into the job, standing in front of a chiller showing a fault code he has never seen. The manual is 400 pages long. The engineer who knew this machine retired last spring.

          The decision

          Suggest causes for this machine, not machines in general. One step at a time, with the reason shown, and every “didn’t fix it” improves the next suggestion.

          Read the full case studyFieldMate · AI · Field service management · 9 months
          Project
          FieldMate, an AI copilot in a field service app for 1,800 HVAC and lift engineers
          My role
          Lead UX designer
          Team
          2 designers, 1 researcher, 2 data scientists, 7 engineers, field supervisors
          Method
          14 ride-alongs, 600 job reports analysed, testing on site with gloves and plant-room noise

          01 · The problem

          Engineers fixed faults from memory, and that memory retired with the senior engineers.

          The first-time fix rate had stalled. Junior engineers phoned senior colleagues from the roof, and the manuals were 400-page PDFs on a phone screen.

          02 · What the research said

          Engineers did not want a chatbot. They had gloves on, a noisy plant room and one free hand. They wanted the next most likely cause for this specific machine, and to know why the copilot thought so.

          “Don’t tell me what a chiller is. Tell me what this one did last time.”Senior engineer, ride-along 6

          03 · What we tried

          • KeptLikely causes ranked from this asset’s history and similar fixes across the fleet, each with its reason.
          • KeptOne step at a time, large targets, and steps read aloud.
          • Kept“Didn’t fix it” moves to the next cause, and is recorded so the next suggestion is better.
          • DroppedFree-text chat. Nobody types with gloves on.
          • DroppedOrdering parts automatically from the top suggestion. A wrong part costs a second visit.

          04 · What shipped

          The copilot opens with the fault code and the asset’s history, ranks likely causes, and guides the engineer one step at a time. When the job is done, it drafts the report from what actually happened, for the engineer to check and sign.

          05 · What changed

          • 81%first-time fix rate, up from 68%
          • −45%calls to senior engineers for help
          • 12 minless paperwork per job

          06 · What I’d do differently

          I’d make the veteran engineers authors, not only testers. Their notes became the best training data, and once they were credited, the copilot felt like theirs.

          Method
          20 interviews, 1,400 helpdesk tickets analysed, a six-week pilot with 600 employees

          01 · The problem

          The answer existed. It was in four places, and three of them were out of date.

          Employees spent hours a week searching the intranet. HR and IT helpdesks answered the same questions again and again, and the old search confidently returned pages nobody had touched in years.

          02 · What the research said

          People trusted a colleague over a document, because a colleague would say “I’m not sure, check with Dev”. Trust came from knowing where an answer came from, how old it was, and who owned it.

          “I don’t want the answer. I want the answer I won’t get in trouble for.”Operations analyst, interview 11

          03 · What we tried

          • KeptEvery answer cites its sources, with the owner and the date each was last updated.
          • KeptWhen sources disagree, both are shown and the owner who can settle it is named.
          • KeptAnswers follow existing permissions. If you can’t see a document, neither can the assistant, and it tells you who can help.
          • DroppedA confident single answer with its sources tucked behind a link.
          • DroppedAnswering from the model’s general knowledge when no company source was found.

          04 · What shipped

          An assistant in the company chat tool and the intranet that answers only from company sources, with citations inline. Old sources are flagged. Conflicts are sent to the document owners, so the fix happens in the source, not in the answer.

          05 · What changed

          • −38%repeat HR and IT tickets
          • 212conflicting policy pages found and fixed in the first quarter
          • 11 minsaved per question, measured in the pilot

          06 · What I’d do differently

          I’d design for document owners as a second user from the start. The assistant was only ever as good as the pages behind it.
